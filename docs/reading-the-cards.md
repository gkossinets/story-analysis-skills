# Reading the cards

A guide to the scene cards produced by the `crews-cards` skill: what each field means, what the symbols stand for, and how to use a deck on a wall. The examples come from the worked demo, [*The Gift of the Magi*](../examples/gift-of-the-magi/).

Each term is explained here as it appears on a card. The full definitions of the twelve CREWS dimensions are in [`crews-analysis/SKILL.md`](../crews-analysis/SKILL.md).

---

## The deck

Every deck is in the same order:

1. **Cover**: the work as a whole (premise, structural characters, stance, shape).
2. **Key**: a one-card legend of the symbols, so a deck on a wall explains itself.
3. **Scene cards**: one per scene, in reading order (the order of the text, not the order of events in the story). Thin **divider cards** (`── Act II ──`) mark acts or sequences when the work has them.
4. **Rhythm map**: the patterns that show up only across the whole work.

Two formats share this structure:

- **Micro cards (3×5 in)** are compressed to the structural spine, for laying out a whole work at once.
- **Letter cards (8.5×11 in or A4)** give one dimension per line, for close work on individual scenes.

Every card in a deck uses the same type size (8pt micro and 11pt letter, or 10pt and 12pt in large print). A card that needs more room continues onto the next card, headed with its title and **(cont.)**. The cover and the rhythm map often do; a scene card should not.

## Anatomy of a micro card

Scene 05 from the demo:

```
05 — The flat, 7 p.m.: Jim comes home
Della, Jim
Jim stares at her cropped head with an unreadable look; she pleads; he embraces her.
A→B→C: reassurance → bewilderment, then the embrace (non-reversal: the feared rejection never comes)
Function: the expected climax, defused
↓ Jim's stare; "This dark assertion will be illuminated later on"
↑ Della's prayer (04)
Mood: bewildered, then tender
Hook: the narrator looks away and promises a payoff; the embrace becomes a cliffhanger
```

| Line | What it gives you |
|---|---|
| **NN — Place, time** | Scene number in reading order, and the setting. Prose works are numbered as if they were screenplays (where the slug lines would fall), so chapters and scenes don't necessarily coincide. |
| Characters | Only those physically present. Absent characters who matter appear in the action line or the plants. |
| Action | One sentence: what happens and what changes. It orients; the A→B→C carries the beats. |
| **A→B→C** | The scene's dramatic movement (below). |
| **Function** | The scene's job in the whole (below). |
| **↓** | Plants opened in this scene. |
| **↑** | Payoffs of earlier plants, with the scene where each was planted. |
| **Mood** | The scene's emotional weather. |
| **Hook** | Why the scene holds attention in its own right. |

Lines that aren't live are left out. A sparse card is a signal about the scene, not a gap in the analysis: a scene with no plants and no payoffs is self-contained, which is worth knowing.

## The A→B→C pattern

**A** is what a character goes for in the scene. **B** is what they get instead. **C** is how they change in response, within the same scene, if they do.

```
A→B→C: a worthy present → months of saving made $1.87 (reveal) → a secret resolve, shown only through her body
```

The tag in parentheses after B says what kind of movement it is:

- **Reveal (r):** the situation turns out not to be what it seemed. In 01, Della learns her months of saving have produced $1.87.
- **Reversal (R):** the terms of the situation change. In 06, Della holds out the chain and learns the watch it was bought for is gone.
- **Non-reversal (N):** the story prepares a reversal and then withholds it. Scene 04 builds toward Jim's verdict on Della's hair; in 05 the rejection never comes. A non-reversal often sits where a conventional climax would be, and is usually deliberate.

Not every scene has all three letters. **A→B** scenes move the story on; **A→B→C** scenes are where characters change. Occasionally a character simply gets what they want; the card then says so ("fulfilment"), as in 03, where Della finds a chain "even worthy of The Watch". A clean fulfilment is often a setup for a later reversal.

The C can belong to the work rather than a character. In the demo's coda (07) the reversal is the narrator's verdict on the couple ("foolish" becomes "wisest"), not anything that happens to them.

## Plants and payoffs: ↓ and ↑

A **plant** (also called a dangling cause) is anything that throws attention forward: a promise, threat, warning, hope, fear, prediction, or an object made conspicuous. A **payoff** closes one.

```
↓ the chain needs the watch; the leather strap; 87 cents left
↑ The Watch (01)
```

- **↓** lists what this scene opens.
- **↑** lists what it closes, with the planting scene's number in parentheses. Numbers are bare: `(01)`, `(01, 03)`.
- The rhythm map draws the full **dangling-cause map**, pairing each plant with its payoff: `↓ watch (01) → ↑ chain bought (03); sold (06)`.
- Plants that are never paid off are listed under **Unresolved**. Some are loose ends; some are deliberate (in the demo, Jim's missing overcoat and gloves).

Micro cards carry about three of each, chosen for structural weight. The analysis file has the complete list.

## Function

The scene's job in the larger structure.

| Term | Job |
|---|---|
| **setup** | establishes the world, the situation and what's at stake |
| **inciting** | the event that sets the main action going |
| **rising** | escalation: stakes or pressure increase |
| **twist** | the situation is turned; what came before must be reread |
| **climax** | the decisive confrontation or turn |
| **release** | tension discharges after a climax |
| **bridge** | connective tissue between larger movements |
| **reconciliation** | elements brought into a new relation at the end (the closing movement of kishotenketsu) |

A scene can have two (`setup; inciting`), and the card may say something more specific when that's more accurate (`the expected climax, defused`). The vocabulary is a tool, not a template; it covers conflict-driven and non-conflict story shapes alike.

---

## Mood and hook

**Mood** is the scene's emotional weather in a phrase. It combines the characters' interior state with the atmosphere made by setting, light, sound or weather, and notes when the two pull against each other ("comic narration over real misery").

**Hook** (on letter cards, **Compelling because**) answers a separate question: setting its structural job aside, why is this scene worth watching or reading? It's an evaluative check. A scene can do its plot job and still be dull; the hook line is where that shows. It matters most for small connective scenes, which writers tend to underserve.

## Letter cards: the extra fields

Letter cards split A→B→C onto separate lines (**A**, **B**, **C**) and add:

| Field | What it gives you |
|---|---|
| **Present** | characters physically present |
| **Format/ritual** | the social structure the scene borrows from life (a sale, a trial, a dinner, a homecoming), with its built-in roles and rules; worth watching where characters conform to it or break it |
| **Stakes** | what is at risk, and whether it rises or falls |
| **Subtext** | the gap between what characters say and what they think or feel |
| **Knowledge** | who knows what; above all, what the reader knows that a character doesn't (dramatic irony), or the reverse |
| **Plants ↓ / Payoffs ↑** | as above |

## The cover card

The cover answers "Who hired the CREWS?", the question of what the work as a whole is built to do.

| Field | What it gives you |
|---|---|
| **Premise** | the story in one sentence |
| **Characters** | the structural characters only, each as *wants → gets (→ transforms)*: the A→B→C pattern scaled up to the whole work. Where that frame would distort a character, another defining dimension is used (type, status, function as a force). |
| **Implied reader** | who the work is for, and what knowledge and expectations it assumes |
| **Implied author** | the stance the work's craft choices construct, which may differ from the narrator's |
| **Tone** | how the work is oriented toward its material |
| **Story shape** | the overall form: three-act, kishotenketsu, episodic, spiralling, or other |
| **Key motifs** | recurring images, objects, phrases |
| **Formats used** | the borrowed social structures the work keeps returning to |
| **Control level** | forces that direct events without being affected by them: gods, fate, the state, the market, an ideology, a narrator who manages what the reader may know |
| **Intended effect** | what the reader is meant to know, feel or recognise by the end |

## The rhythm map

```
r r r r N R R
```

One letter per scene, in reading order: **r** reveal (scene 01's setup counts as a small reveal), **R** reversal, **N** non-reversal. Things to look for:

- **Long runs of r** without an R can feel flat.
- **Clusters of R** can tip into melodrama, unless the reversals work on different axes. In the demo, 06 reverses the couple's fortune and 07 reverses the story's judgment of them.
- **An N where a climax would be** is usually the work's most deliberate structural choice.

The rest of the card summarises the work's **suspense**, its **dangling-cause map** and **unresolved** plants, the **status and power arcs** of the main characters, and the **mood arc**. Suspense comes in three modes:

- **Postponement:** the reader waits for an outcome they know is coming.
- **Screening:** the point of view blocks information the reader knows must exist.
- **Misdirection:** the information is in plain sight but flagged as something else.

## Other conventions

- **(cont.)** in a card's title: the card continues the one before it.
- **[orbit]** after a number: a unit of non-linear prose that couldn't be cut into conventional scenes, analysed as a "temporal orbit" (a stretch the narration keeps circling back to) instead.
- **── Act II ──**: a divider card between acts or sequences.
- **Scene numbers** follow reading order. A flashback gets the next number, not a number from its place in the chronology.

---

## Using a deck on a wall

- **Lay the scene cards out in reading order**, one row per act or sequence if the work has them. Put the cover and key at the head and the rhythm map at the end.
- **Read along the A→B→C lines** first. They're the spine of each card, and a run of them shows the work's dramatic movement at a glance.
- **Connect the plants and payoffs**, with string, a pencilled line or matching coloured dots, using the ↓ and ↑ lines and the dangling-cause map. Long arcs, unanswered plants and payoffs with no visible plant (a sign of something introduced at the moment it's needed) all show up.
- **Mark the r, R and N** in a corner of each card to see the rhythm map in place, or set the reversals slightly out of line.
- **Read the Function and Mood lines across a row** to see where the pace and atmosphere change, and where adjacent scenes do the same job.
- **For your own work in progress**, the Hook line is the one to look at hardest: a scene whose hook is thin is a candidate for cutting, merging or rethinking.
