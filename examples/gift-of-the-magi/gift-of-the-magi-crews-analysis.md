# CREWS analysis: The Gift of the Magi by O. Henry

## Overview

**Premise:** On Christmas Eve a poor young married couple each secretly sell their most prized possession to buy the other a gift, and each sale makes the other's gift useless.

**Characters:**
- Della — protagonist and focal character; wants a present worthy of Jim; gets the fob chain, made useless by his sale of the watch (→ her sacrifice is matched, not wasted)
- Jim — her husband, held offstage until 05; wants to give Della the combs she has longed for; gets them to her, useless without her hair (→ meets the double loss with calm and comic understatement)
- Madame Sofronie — gatekeeper; converts the hair into cash; one scene
- The narrator — intrusive commentator; controls what the reader knows and when, then turns the plot's verdict ("foolish") into the moral one ("wisest")

**Implied reader:** an early-1900s American newspaper reader who knows the Nativity story, New York prices and places, and the conventions of the sentimental Christmas tale

**Implied author:** affectionate and ironic toward everything except the couple's love, which is finally exempted from irony

**Tone:** comic-sentimental; mock-heroic narration over real distress, turning homiletic in the coda

**Story shape:** twist-ending arc with no antagonist; maps equally well onto kishotenketsu (01 introduction; 02–04 development; 05–06 twist; 07 reconciliation)

**Key motifs:** money as exact figures ($1.87, $8, $20, $21, 87 cents); the two treasures (hair and watch); the mirror; royal and biblical treasure (Sheba, Solomon, the magi); counting ("the hairs of my head were numbered"); the chops

**Formats used:** Christmas gift-giving; the commercial sale and purchase; the evening homecoming and dinner

**Control level:** the wage economy ($20 a week), implicit and never argued with; the calendar obligation of Christmas; the narrator, who stage-manages what the reader may know

**Intended effect:** the reader laughs at the futility of the two gifts and is then asked to see the futile sacrifices as the true gift

## Scene-by-scene

### Scene 01 — The Youngs' flat, afternoon of Christmas Eve

**Present:** Della

**Action:** Della counts $1.87, cries, and at the pier-glass resolves (without saying so) to sell her hair.

**Characters**
- Della: characterised by action rather than description (counting three times, howling); impulsive, then suddenly decisive
- Jim: present only as a name on a card and as the object of Della's longing

**Relationships**
- Status: household decline is written into the name card; "Dillingham" blurs as income falls from $30 to $20 a week
- Status: inside the flat, affection overrides money ("greatly hugged")
- Subtext: the decision is never stated; we get shining eyes, lost colour, a tear on the worn red carpet

**Engine**
- Circumstances: $1.87; Christmas tomorrow; an $8 flat with a dead letter-box and a dead bell
- Stasis and intrusion: stasis is genteel poverty; the deadline of Christmas turns ordinary scarcity into a crisis
- A→B→C: wants a worthy present → finds months of saving have produced $1.87 (reveal) → resolves on the sacrifice, shown only through her body
- Stakes: moral and emotional rather than material; whether she can give something "worthy of the honour of being owned by Jim"
- Knowledge: the two treasures are introduced together at the exact moment she lets her hair down, so the reader infers the plan before it is acted on
- Plants ↓: the two treasures, hair and the gold watch; Sheba and Solomon as images of royal treasure; the $20 weekly wage

**Writing**
- Perspective: intrusive omniscient narrator; leaves Della mid-cry to address the reader ("take a look at the home") and returns
- Information: exposition delivered as a tour while the action is paused; the treasures' exposition is timed to work as a plant
- Mood: comic narration over real misery; grey cat, grey fence, grey backyard as atmosphere

**Scene function**
- Function: setup and inciting
- What changes: from helpless counting to a secret resolve
- Compelling because: the narrator's jokes ("sobs, sniffles, and smiles") keep the pathos from going maudlin, and the unspoken decision makes the reader a co-conspirator

### Scene 02 — Mme. Sofronie's, one flight up, same afternoon

**Present:** Della, Madame Sofronie

**Action:** Della sells her hair for twenty dollars.

**Characters**
- Madame: a type; large, too white, chilly; does not live up to her French trade name
- Della: brisk to the point of rudeness ("Give it to me quick")

**Relationships**
- Status: Madame sets the price unilaterally; Della does not bargain
- Format/ritual: a commercial transaction cut down to four lines of dialogue
- Subtext: Della's haste keeps her from feeling the loss

**Engine**
- A→B→C: wants money → gets $20 without complication (reveal of the plan in action); the only scene in which a want is met cleanly
- Stakes: the cut is irreversible; after this scene the choice can't be undone
- Knowledge: one-sided dramatic irony begins; Jim will not know what the reader knows
- Payoffs ↑: hair as treasure (01) converted into cash; "the brown cascade" repeats 01's imagery

**Writing**
- Perspective: external, almost all dialogue; the narrator's usual commentary drops away
- Information: the cutting itself is elided
- Mood: cold, commercial, fast

**Scene function**
- Function: rising; the point of no return
- What changes: Della has money and no longer has her treasure
- Compelling because: after the digressive 01, the speed of the sale lands as a shock

### Scene 03 — The stores, the next two hours

**Present:** Della

**Action:** Della searches the shops and buys a platinum fob chain for Jim's watch for $21.

**Characters**
- Della: her love is expressed as taste; she judges the chain by Jim's qualities ("Quietness and value")

**Relationships**
- Status: the chain is meant to raise Jim's public standing; he looks at the watch "on the sly" because of its old leather strap

**Engine**
- A→B→C: wants a gift worthy of Jim → finds one "even worthy of The Watch" (fulfilment, not reversal)
- Stakes: whether her sacrifice can be redeemed in an object
- Knowledge: misdirection; the watch is foregrounded as the reason the gift is perfect, never as something Jim might part with
- Plants ↓: the chain depends on the watch; the leather strap and Jim's embarrassment; 87 cents left
- Payoffs ↑: The Watch (01)

**Writing**
- Perspective: close to Della; free indirect ("It surely had been made for Jim and no one else")
- Information: two hours compressed into summary; the narrator mocks his own "hashed metaphor"
- Mood: euphoric ("rosy wings")

**Scene function**
- Function: rising
- What changes: money becomes an object; anxiety becomes elation
- Compelling because: the more exactly the chain fits the watch, the harder the later reversal will hit; the scene's satisfaction is the trap

### Scene 04 — The flat, early evening to 7 o'clock

**Present:** Della

**Action:** Della curls her cropped hair, fears Jim's reaction, readies dinner and waits by the door, praying.

**Characters**
- Della: self-mocking ("a Coney Island chorus girl") but frightened; the prayer shows the real stake is her desirability to Jim

**Relationships**
- Subtext: "If Jim doesn't kill me" is comic hyperbole carrying real fear

**Engine**
- Circumstances: household routine (coffee made, chops ready, Jim never late)
- A→B→C: wants to repair "the ravages made by generosity added to love" → gets curls that make her look like "a truant schoolboy" (partial) → reduces her hope to a prayer
- Stakes: at their peak; whether Jim will still find her pretty
- Knowledge: suspense by postponement; the reader waits for Jim's reaction to a known fact
- Plants ↓: "Please God, make him think I am still pretty"; the chops

**Writing**
- Perspective: close to Della; the narrator addresses "dear friends"
- Information: the mirror returns (01), now showing loss
- Mood: anxious domestic quiet; time narrowing toward 7

**Scene function**
- Function: rising; the wait before the expected climax
- What changes: elation turns to dread
- Compelling because: it builds suspense toward the wrong climax (Jim's verdict on her hair), which the story will step around

### Scene 05 — The flat, 7 p.m.: Jim comes home

**Present:** Della, Jim

**Action:** Jim stares at Della's cropped head with an expression she cannot read; she pleads; he wakes from his trance and embraces her.

**Characters**
- Jim: seen at last; thin, serious, twenty-two, without overcoat or gloves; poverty shown on his body
- Della: chatter as defence; "I'm me without my hair, ain't I?"

**Relationships**
- Status: Della's fear hands all power to Jim's gaze; he holds the verdict
- Subtext: his stare hides something; neither Della nor the reader can read it
- Format/ritual: the homecoming (wife by the door, dinner ready) is disrupted

**Engine**
- A→B→C: wants reassurance → gets bewilderment ("You say your hair is gone?"), then the embrace (reveal: it isn't anger) → a new question opens rather than a change in Della
- Stakes: shift from Della's looks to an open question about Jim
- Knowledge: the dramatic irony flips; Jim now knows something that Della and the reader don't
- Plants ↓: Jim's unreadable stare; "This dark assertion will be illuminated later on" (an explicit promise from the narrator)
- Payoffs ↑: Della's prayer (04), apparently answered

**Writing**
- Perspective: still Della's side; Jim is seen only from outside
- Information: screening; the narrator looks away during the embrace ("let us regard with discreet scrutiny some inconsequential object") and inserts the magi riddle in the gap
- Mood: bewildered and tense, then tender

**Scene function**
- Function: the expected climax resolved early (non-reversal)
- What changes: Della's fear is answered; a puzzle replaces it
- Compelling because: the reader shares Della's inability to read Jim, and the narrator's open promise turns the embrace into a cliffhanger

### Scene 06 — The flat, continuous: the gifts

**Present:** Della, Jim

**Action:** Jim gives Della the tortoise-shell combs she can no longer wear; she gives him the chain; he reveals that he sold the watch to buy the combs.

**Characters**
- Della: joy, then tears, then recovery ("My hair grows so fast, Jim!")
- Jim: calm and practical in loss; deflates the moment ("suppose you put the chops on")

**Relationships**
- Status: symmetry; each has sacrificed equally, so neither holds power over the other
- Format/ritual: the gift exchange is completed and voided at once

**Engine**
- A→B→C: Della wants Jim to see his present → learns the watch is gone (reversal; the second of two) → the gifts are put away "a while" and dinner resumes
- Stakes: the material loss is now total and shared; the emotional stake is settled
- Knowledge: recognition; Della and the reader catch up at the same moment, and Jim's stare (05) is explained after the fact
- Payoffs ↑: the watch (01, 03); the chain (03); Jim's stare (05); the chops (04); Della's prayer, now answered in words ("anything … could make me like my girl any less")

**Writing**
- Perspective: still Della's side; Jim's account arrives as three plain sentences, the barest prose in the story
- Information: the combs are introduced at the moment of payoff ("that Della had worshipped for long in a Broadway window"), with no earlier plant
- Mood: ecstatic scream, hysterical tears, then quiet comedy

**Scene function**
- Function: twist and climax
- What changes: both gifts are made useless; the marriage is revealed as mutual
- Compelling because: the second reversal mirrors the first so exactly that it lands as a joke and a revelation at once; Jim's turn to dinner keeps it from melodrama

### Scene 07 — Coda: the narrator on the magi

**Present:** none (the narrator speaks)

**Action:** The narrator explains who the magi were and declares the "two foolish children" the wisest of all givers.

**Engine**
- A→B→C: the narrator first calls the chronicle "uneventful" and the couple foolish → then reverses his own verdict (reversal of judgment rather than fortune)
- Payoffs ↑: "This dark assertion will be illuminated later on" (05); Sheba and Solomon's treasures (01); the title

**Writing**
- Perspective: the narrator in his own voice, with no character present
- Information: the moral is declared, not dramatised; repetition ("wisest … wisest … wisest") does the persuading
- Mood: from wry to benedictory

**Scene function**
- Function: reconciliation and moral
- What changes: practical failure is reclassified as moral success; the reader's laughter is reclassified with it
- Compelling because: the narrator stages a reversal of judgment that mirrors the plot's reversal of fortune, ending on a short sentence that names the couple with the title

## Cross-scene patterns

**Reveal/reversal rhythm:**
- 01 — the flat, $1.87: **setup**
- 02 — Mme. Sofronie's: **reveal** (the plan in action; the hair is sold)
- 03 — the stores: **reveal** (the chain; Jim's embarrassment over the strap)
- 04 — the wait: **reveal** (the real stake is Della's desirability)
- 05 — Jim's homecoming: **non-reversal** (the rejection 04 has prepared does not come)
- 06 — the gifts: **reversal** (double: combs without hair, chain without watch)
- 07 — coda: **reversal** (the narrator's verdict turns from foolish to wisest)

The story runs four low-key movements and then spends all its reversals at once. The non-reversal at 05 is what makes the cluster work: by defusing the climax the reader was expecting, it clears the ground for the one they weren't. Two reversals back to back (06, 07) would risk melodrama, but they work on different axes (fortune, then judgment).

**Dangling cause map:**
- ↓ Della's hair, one of two treasures (01) → ↑ sold (02); combs useless (06)
- ↓ Jim's gold watch, the other treasure (01) → ↑ chain bought for it (03); sold for the combs (06)
- ↓ the leather strap; Jim checks the time "on the sly" (03) → ↑ the chain is useless (06)
- ↓ "Please God, make him think I am still pretty" (04) → ↑ the embrace (05); "could make me like my girl any less" (06)
- ↓ Jim's unreadable stare (05) → ↑ explained by the combs (06)
- ↓ "This dark assertion will be illuminated later on" (05) → ↑ the magi coda (07)
- ↓ Sheba's jewels, Solomon's treasures (01) → ↑ the magi's gifts (07)
- ↓ the chops (04) → ↑ "suppose you put the chops on" (06)

**Suspense:**
- Postponement in 04–05: the reader waits for Jim's reaction to a known fact
- Screening in 05: Jim's stare and the narrator's averted gaze block his side of the story
- Misdirection across 01–04: the narration stays with Della, so her secret looks like the only one, and the watch is flagged as what the chain completes

**Unresolved:**
- Jim's need for an overcoat and gloves (05)
- The 87 cents (03)
- The combs are put away "a while"; the hair will grow back (a soft promise of later use), but the watch is gone for good

**Status and power arcs:**
- Della: holds information power (her secret) through 01–04; becomes dependent on Jim's verdict at 04–05; ends an equal giver at 06
- Jim: a name on a card with the positional power of the earner (01); holds all the power of judgment at 05; gives it up with his own confession at 06, ending as a gently mocked "lord of the flat"
- The narrator: controls knowledge throughout; at 07 converts that control into moral authority

**Mood arc:**
- Grey misery (01) → cold commerce (02) → euphoria (03) → dread (04)
- Bewilderment, then tenderness (05) → hysteria, then calm comedy (06) → benediction (07)

**Structural devices:**
- Symmetry: two treasures planted together, two sales, two gifts, two reversals
- Exact prices throughout, so the sacrifice has a measurable size
- The mirror (01, 04): first a source of pride, then of fear
- The narrator's asides, used both to manage pace and to steer judgment

**Tempo and density:**
- 01 slow and digressive; 02 very fast; 03 summary
- 04 slow; 05–06 dramatised in near real time; 07 direct address

## Who hired the CREWS?

**Implied reader:** a turn-of-the-century urban newspaper reader who knows the Nativity, the price of a furnished flat, Coney Island and the Broadway shop windows, and who expects both a sentimental Christmas story and a twist. What is left out is Jim's entire day: the narration never follows him, so the reader never wonders where his money came from. The story also leaves out any argument about the $20 wage; poverty is the setting, not the subject.

**Implied author:** affectionate and ironic. The mock-elevated diction and self-mocking asides ironise the flat, the shop, the narrator's own metaphors and the couple's foolishness, but never their love; in the coda the irony is withdrawn entirely. The stance is sentimental-moral, with wit as cover for the sentiment.

**Tone:** comic-sentimental; ironic through 06, homiletic in 07.

**Message or intended effect:** that sacrificial love is the real gift, and that practical futility doesn't cancel its value. Note what kind of claim this is: the plot shows two sacrifices cancelling each other, which is equally compatible with "foolish" and "wisest"; the story settles the question by declaration and repetition in the coda, not by anything the plot demonstrates. The narrator even lets the reader hold the "foolish" reading for a sentence before overruling it.

**Craft choices that construct these positions:**
- Single-side focalisation: the narration stays with Della and keeps Jim offstage until 05; this makes the reader share her secret and assume it is the only one, so the symmetry of the two sacrifices is discovered rather than designed, which is the emotional point.
- An intrusive narrator who openly manages knowledge: he looks away at the key moment and promises a payoff ("will be illuminated later on"); this makes the twist feel fair while delaying it, and casts the reader as one of "the wise" who will be told the meaning.
- Symmetrical construction: the two treasures are planted in a single paragraph (01) and paid off in a single scene (06); equal losses read as a shared joke rather than a tragedy, which supports the claim that mutuality, not the objects, is what is given.
- Exact prices: the sums make the sacrifice concrete and measurable; the story then declares love uncountable ("nobody could ever count my love for you"), so the arithmetic sets up the moral by contrast.

**Transparency:** mixed. The apparatus is openly exposed: the narrator comments on his own metaphors, addresses the reader, and announces a payoff in advance. Outcomes follow from setup (the watch is planted in 01 and 03), with one seam: the combs appear only at the moment of payoff. Focalisation explains why Jim's purchase is hidden, but the combs are described as something Della had long "worshipped" in a window, a longing the close narration could have shown earlier and didn't. The twist depends on that omission.

**Story shape:** a Western twist-ending arc (setup, rising action, a false climax at 05, a double reversal at 06), but with no antagonist; the obstacle is poverty and the engine is the distribution of knowledge. The four movements map just as cleanly onto kishotenketsu, and the ending (a twist followed by reconciliation rather than a conflict resolved) arguably fits that shape better than a conflict-climax model.
